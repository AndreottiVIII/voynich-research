\begin{abstract}
\setlength{\parskip}{0.7em}
\noindent The Voynich manuscript (Yale, Beinecke MS~408; vellum radiocarbon-dated to 1404--1438) is written in a script
that has never been read. We do not propose a decipherment. Instead, we describe \emph{how} the text is written, using
more than six hundred preregistered, reproducible experiments, each first validated on synthetic texts in which the
effect is known to be present or absent, and replicated on three independent transliterations. We then compare every
property with the same measurement on about one hundred natural languages, eight constructed languages, handwritten
gibberish produced by volunteers, published Voynich-like generators and ciphers, the eighteenth-century Copiale cipher,
and---for the first time in a systematic way---79 real medieval scribes: six Nordic manuscripts (c.~1200--1550) and 73
German manuscripts (1350--1500) transcribed at the level of letter forms, with their original lines and pages.

\noindent\textbf{New findings.} (1)~The text behaves as a \emph{chain of glyphs} in which the space is a weak boundary:
spaces are predictable from the two surrounding glyphs, re-cutting the chain at other permitted points mostly yields
words attested elsewhere, and the rules linking the end of one word to the start of the next mirror the rules inside
words more than in any of 71 languages. (2)~Adjacent words are linked by \emph{junction rules} (\eva{qo-} after
\eva{-y/-o/-d}, \eva{o-} after \eva{-n/-r/-s/-m}; \eva{-l/-r} chosen by the next word), applied while writing and looking
exactly one word ahead. (3)~The \emph{line is a closed unit}: the junction stops at the line break and at the gap left
by a drawing, and line starts and ends have their own forms. (4)~The scribe \emph{copies from the line immediately
above}, adapting the copies to the junction rules. (5)~Each line \emph{avoids starting like the line above}. (6)~Binary
choices between variant forms (\eva{k/t}, \eva{sh/ch}, \eva{-ey/-dy}) follow a \emph{short-lived state} that persists
for two to three words regardless of which word is written; it is not copying, not position, separate for each choice,
and identical across scribal hands---a rule of the system. (7)~Marked variants \emph{drift} towards simpler forms along
the line. Two further properties are quantified anew: immediate repetition of the previous word (1.0\% of adjacent
pairs; 3.9\% with one glyph changed) and a strong page, bifolium and paragraph structure.

\noindent\textbf{Comparisons.} Junction rules, left-margin avoidance, line-edge forms and immediate repetition have no
counterpart in the 79 scribes (they are absent or four to a hundred times weaker). The short state and the in-line drift
were not found in the six Nordic scribes (the measurement on the 73 German scribes is in progress), and the Copiale
copyist does the opposite of the Voynich, rotating homophones between adjacent words. A slower state crossing line
breaks, by contrast, is normal in real scribes. No substitution reading in tens of languages, none of the published
readings we could test (Bax, Vatne, Cheshire, Schechter, Gatta), none of the simulated historical mechanisms (homophone
choice, Alberti's index letters, Bacon's biliteral cipher, Trithemius' word tables, a line-restarting autokey), no
constructed language and no published generator reproduces these properties together.

\noindent\textbf{Conclusion.} The Voynich text is the product of a writing system with rules between neighbouring words
and at line edges far stronger than those of any scribe, language or generator we tested. These rules do not reveal what
the text says, but they exclude many explanations and provide a checklist that any future proposal must reproduce.
\end{abstract}
